\begin{center}
    {\LARGE \textbf{Abstract}}
\end{center}
\vspace{5mm}

Light Up, also known as Akari, is a Japanese puzzle game. Its rules are simple, but its solutions are hard to find. The player places light bulbs on a grid of black and white cells until every white cell is lit, while following a small set of rules. This makes it a useful problem for comparing different algorithms.

In this project Light Up is treated as a computational problem rather than as a game. Five solvers were implemented and compared under the same conditions. Three of them are backtracking solvers that differ only in how much reasoning is done during the search. The other two are local search solvers, hill climbing and simulated annealing. All five were tested on the same 75 generated puzzles.

The main result is that reasoning matters more than speed. The solver with full constraint propagation solved every generated puzzle, and it solved a published puzzle without searching at all. Simulated annealing was clearly better than hill climbing, which confirms earlier work. Puzzles with fewer clues also turned out to be easier for the solvers, even though they are harder for people.


\vspace{2mm}
\textbf{Keywords:}  light up, akari, constraint satisfaction, local search, hill climbing, backtracking, simulated annealing, SAT, CSP, DFS

\chapter{Introduction}
\section{Background of the Light Up puzzle}
\indent 

Light Up is a binary determination puzzle published by \textbf{Nikoli}\cite{nikoli}, which is a Japanese publisher focused on logical games and puzzles. As of 2011, three full books consisting of only Light Up puzzles have been published by this publisher.

The puzzle is also interesting as a computing problem. McPhail\cite{mcphail} proved that deciding whether a Light Up puzzle has a solution is NP-complete, using a reduction from Circuit-SAT. This means that in the worst case the solving time grows very fast as the board gets bigger. No algorithm can remove this limit. A better algorithm can only push it further away. Measuring how far each method pushes it is the goal of this project.



\section{Rules}

The rules of the game are rather simple. Light Up is played on a rectangular board of black and white cells; all the instances used in this project are square, of size $N \times N$. The player places light bulbs in white cells. A bulb sends rays of light horizontally and  vertically, illuminating its entire row and column unless the light is blocked by a black cell. No two bulbs may shine on each other, meaning no two bulbs should be on the same row or column, unless blocked from each other by a black cell.  Black cells might have numbers written on them (from 0 up to 4), which indicate the number of bulbs that should be placed adjacent to them. Two cells are considered adjacent if they share a common edge. Blank black cells might have any number of bulbs adjacent to them. The player solves the puzzle, if they manage to light up all the white cells while following all the rules(Figure \ref{fig:example}).

\chapter{Literature Review}

This literature review evaluates some approaches that have been used for solving the Light Up puzzle, including backtracking, constraint propagation, local search, and satisfiability.

\indent

\section{Hill Climbing and Simulated Annealing}

Libo Sun, James Browning, Roberto Perera in their paper "Shedding some light on light up with artificial intelligence" \cite{light_up_ai} solved the puzzle using an approach involving heuristic search. The authors tested \textit{Hill Climbing} and \textit{Simulated Annealing} using a fitness function that measured the percentage of lit cells while penalizing "conflicts", such as bulbs shining on each other or incorrect numbers of bulbs around black cells. 

To formalize the problem, the board was represented as a two-dimensional array of single digits. Each digit stands for one cell state: a black cell with a given number, a blank black cell, an empty white cell, a cell with a bulb, a lit cell, or a cell where no bulb may be placed (Figure \ref{fig:boardarray}).
\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\linewidth]{Screenshot 2026-07-23 at 16.15.38.png}
    \caption{Example of board representation as a numerical array\cite{light_up_ai}}
    \label{fig:boardarray}
\end{figure}


At the initial state the bulbs are placed randomly. The available actions are adding a bulb, removing a bulb, and moving a bulb to another empty cell. The authors first implemented plain hill climbing. At each step the algorithm generates a set of neighbouring states and moves to the best one. If no neighbour is better than the current state, the algorithm stops. This often leaves it stuck in a \textbf{local maximum}. They also tested an improved initialization, which places bulbs in cells where only one choice is possible and so reduces the search space. Finally, they compared a \textbf{2-action model} (add or remove) with a \textbf{3-action model} (add, remove, or move).

To escape local maxima the authors used Simulated Annealing, which reached \textbf{100\% accuracy} on all 30 test boards. In this method a worse move can still be accepted, with a probability that depends on a "temperature" value. The temperature starts high, so many bad moves are accepted and the search explores widely. It then decreases, so the search becomes stricter and behaves more like hill climbing. The authors reported that simulated annealing performed best, hill climbing with improved initialization came second, and plain hill climbing was the worst. They also found that the 3-action model gave better results than the 2-action model.

\section{SAT and Backtracking}

In his "Analysis of Akari" paper\cite{analysis_akari} Bram Pulles approached the puzzle by developing SAT and Backtracking solver systems. Before developing the solvers, the author executed a trivial solver, which was used for preprocessing that identified certain placements where the number of available spaces around a black cell exactly matches its numerical clue. It also identifies the cells where a bulb cannot be placed due to existing lights or clues with number zero on them(Figure \ref{fig:trivial}).  

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{Screenshot 2026-07-28 at 16.48.10.png}
    \caption{Examples of trivial solutions\cite{analysis_akari}}
    \label{fig:trivial}
\end{figure}

Pulles then implemented a SAT solver that uses the Z3 theorem prover. It translates the whole board into a boolean formula built from three conditions: every numbered cell has the correct number of adjacent bulbs, every white cell is lit at least once, and no row or column segment contains two bulbs. With this encoding the solver finishes 40 × 40 puzzles in less than a second.

The author also implemented a backtracking solver based on depth-first search. It starts from the base solution of the trivial solver and then sorts the remaining candidate cells so that cells next to numbered walls come first, because the number constraints are the hard part. During the search it checks whether a clue can still be satisfied, and backtracks early when it cannot.

The two approaches differ greatly in speed. The SAT solver is much faster on normal puzzles, because Z3 is a highly optimized library, while the backtracking solver can need minutes even for a 14 × 14 board. There is one exception. On nearly empty boards with very few walls the SAT solver becomes slow, and the simpler backtracking solver finishes first.

\section{Relation to this project}

Light Up has also been studied as a constraint problem. Çelik et al.\cite{celik2009} model Akari and three other grid puzzles in both Answer Set Programming and Constraint Programming, and compare the two on how the problem is written and on running time. Their goal is to compare two declarative languages. The goal here is different: to measure how a search procedure behaves.

These works shaped the design of this project in three ways. First, Pulles is the closest earlier work, even though he does not use the words of constraint satisfaction. His trivial solver is constraint propagation. His ordering of candidate cells, which puts cells next to numbered walls first, is a variable-ordering heuristic. His check for a clue that can no longer be satisfied is the same test used by the forward-checking solver here. The difference is that his inference runs only once, before the search starts. In this project the puzzle is written as a CSP, and the same inference is run inside the search, at every node, until nothing changes. The effect of this change is then measured.

Second, Pulles uses the Z3 library for the search itself, so his results mostly describe Z3. Here every solver was written from scratch, which is what makes a comparison of search behaviour useful. Third, the hill climbing and simulated annealing of Sun et al.\ were reimplemented, so that the order they report between the two could be tested on different puzzles.


\chapter{Solution approaches}

\section{CSP}
\label{sec:csp}
The first approach to solving the Light Up puzzle is to formulate it as a Constraint Satisfaction(CSP) problem. A CSP is defined by a set of variables X, a domain of possible values D, and a set of constraints C:

\bigskip
X = \{all white cells on the board\}
\newline 

D = \{bulb, empty\}
\newline

        C = \{C1, C2, C3\}
\bigskip

C1: No two bulbs illuminate each other

C2: Every white cell is illuminated by at least one bulb

C3: Every numbered black cell has exactly the specified number of adjacent bulbs
\bigskip

Consider some definitions to express constraints formally. Let $\text{\textbf{\textit{sight}}}(w)$ be the set of white cells that share its row or column with a white cell $w \in X$ and are not separated from it by a black cell. For a numbered black cell, let $n_c$ denote its clue value and $N_c$ the set of white cells orthogonally adjacent to it. Let $b_w \in \{0, 1\}$ indicate whether a cell has a bulb or not. Finally, let $\text{\textbf{\textit{Clues}}}$ denote the set of numbered black cells on the board.
\newline
Using this notation, the three constraints can be written formally as:
\newline
  
  \(C_1: \quad \forall\, w \in X,\ \forall\, w' \in \text{sight}(w): \quad b_w + b_{w'} \le 1\)
\newline

\(C_2: \quad \forall\, w \in X:           \quad b_w + \sum_{w' \in \text{sight}(w)} b_{w'} \ge 1\)

\bigskip

  \(C_3: \quad \forall\, c \in \text{Clues}: \quad \sum_{x \in N_c} b_x = n_c\)
  
\bigskip
Solving the problem means finding an assignment of values from D to every variable in X that satisfies all the constraints in C. All three constraints are \emph{counting} constraints, because each one limits how many bulbs a region may contain: at least one in a cell's line of sight, at most one in a wall-free segment, and exactly $n_c$ next to a clue. The methods applied to them, backtracking search with forward checking and with constraint propagation, follow Russell and Norvig\cite{aima}. Three backtracking approaches are implemented, all are formed based on the same problem formulation, but differ in how much reasoning they do before and during branching: a naive baseline, backtracking with forward checking, and a full inference. Comparing these three approaches would mean measuring the effect of inference on the search process.

\subsection{Naive Backtracking}

  The naive solver is the baseline. It visits the white cells in a fixed row-major order.
  At each cell it first tries placing a bulb, and only leaves the cell empty if that
  fails. After each attempt it runs a consistency check. If the check passes, the search
  moves to the next cell. If it fails, the attempt is undone and the other value is
  tried. This is a plain depth-first search over the CSP of Section~\ref{sec:csp}. There
  is no variable-ordering heuristic, so cells are taken in reading order even when this
  is a poor choice, and the only value ordering is that a bulb is always tried first.

  The consistency check does not store anything between nodes. It rebuilds everything
  from scratch every time: it checks the current assignment against $C_1$ and $C_3$, and
  recomputes the set of lit cells from the beginning.

  One extra rule is needed to make this solver usable. It is called the
  \emph{doomed-cell} rule here. If a white cell $w$ is not lit, and $w$ and every cell in
  $\text{sight}(w)$ have already been decided empty, then $w$ can never be lit. $C_2$ can
  no longer be satisfied, so the branch is abandoned at once. Without this rule the
  solver would still be correct, because every complete assignment is tested at the end,
  but it would explore huge subtrees that are already hopeless.

  An assignment is accepted only when every white cell has been decided and a final goal
  test confirms all three constraints, including the exact clue counts of $C_3$. Since
  nothing is propagated in advance and nothing is reused between nodes, the cost of this
  solver grows with the number of nodes it visits. The two solvers below are designed to
  reduce that number.


\subsection{Backtracking with Forward Checking}

  The forward-checking and the full-inference solvers use the same search procedure. They
  differ only in how much they infer at each node. This subsection describes what they
  share, and the next one describes what full inference adds.

  Unlike the naive solver, both keep information across the whole search instead of
  rescanning the board. They store the status of each white cell (bulb, empty, or
  undecided), a count of how many bulbs currently light each cell, and a trail of the
  order in which assignments were made. The trail lets the solver undo exactly what
  changed when it backtracks. The geometry of the board, meaning which cells each cell can
  see and which cells are next to each clue, is computed once before the search starts.

  When a bulb is placed, the solver immediately applies $C_1$ and marks every cell in its
  line of sight as empty, because a bulb in any of those cells would conflict with it.
  If one of those cells already holds a bulb, the conflict is found at once instead of
  later.

  Both solvers also use the same variable-ordering heuristic. They first choose an
  undecided neighbour of the clue with the least slack, that is, the clue whose number of
  free neighbours is closest to the number of bulbs it still needs. When every clue is
  finished, they choose a cell that could light the unlit cell with the fewest remaining
  options. This is the usual most-constrained-variable heuristic, adapted to counting
  constraints. A bulb is still tried before an empty cell, because a bulb makes progress
  towards $C_2$ and makes a wrong choice fail sooner.

  The difference between the two solvers is the check made before each recursive call.
  Forward checking only checks, it never assigns. It uses two conditions. The first
  applies to $C_3$. Let $\text{placed}(c)$ be the number of a clue's neighbours that hold
  a bulb, and $\text{free}(c)$ the number that are still undecided. The branch fails as
  soon as $\text{placed}(c) > n_c$ or $\text{placed}(c) + \text{free}(c) < n_c$ for any
  clue $c$. The second applies to $C_2$. If an unlit cell is already decided empty and no
  undecided cell remains in its line of sight, it can never be lit, so the branch fails
  immediately. Neither condition assigns a value. They only decide whether the branch is
  still alive, so this is called pruning rather than inference.


\subsection{Backtracking with Full Inference}

  The full-inference configuration keeps everything described above, but replaces the
  prune-only lookahead with a step that actively assigns values it can prove are forced,
  repeating until a full pass makes no further changes -- i.e., until a fixpoint is
  reached. Three rules drive this:

  \begin{align}
  \text{placed}(c) = n_c &\;\Longrightarrow\; \forall x \in \text{free}(c):\ b_x \leftarrow 0
  &&\text{(saturated clue)} \\
  \text{placed}(c) + |\text{free}(c)| = n_c &\;\Longrightarrow\; \forall x \in \text{free}(c):\ b_x \leftarrow 1
  &&\text{(exhausted clue)} \\
  \bigl|\{x \in \{w\} \cup \text{sight}(w) : x \text{ undecided}\}\bigr| = 1,\ w \text{ unlit}
  &\;\Longrightarrow\; b_x \leftarrow 1 &&\text{(forced lighter)}
  \end{align}

  A saturated clue already has as many bulbs as its number requires, so every remaining
  undecided neighbour must be empty, otherwise $C_3$ would be broken. An exhausted clue is
  the opposite case: so few undecided neighbours remain that all of them must hold a bulb,
  otherwise the clue can never reach its number. A forced lighter applies to $C_2$: if an
  unlit cell has only one remaining cell that could light it, that cell must hold a bulb.

  Applying one rule can create the conditions for another. A forced bulb, for example, may
  make some other clue saturated. For this reason all three rules are repeated in a loop
  until a full pass changes nothing.

  Because this solver assigns values instead of only pruning, a large part of each puzzle
  is solved by logic rather than by branching. These assignments are counted separately as
  propagations, and compared with search nodes in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}. The same
  step also runs once before the first branching decision. This means that the direct
  consequences of the starting clues, such as a clue of 0 forcing all its neighbours to be
  empty, are found before the search begins.


\section{Local search}

Local search was used for the second solution approach. Two solvers were implemented: hill climbing and simulated annealing. Both solvers are implemented as incomplete algorithms that operate on a complete-state formulation, meaning they evaluate entire board configurations unlike the CSP approach. 

Both solvers share several specific components. They navigate the search space using a 3-action model: adding a bulb, removing a bulb, and moving one bulb from one cell to another available cell. 

To ensure efficiency both solvers share an infrastructure that begins by precalculating entire board's geometry. Before starting the search, the program determines which white cells can see each other across the board's rows and columns. This allows the agent to immediately know which cells are illuminated every time a move is made instead of scanning the board every time. This significantly speeds up the evaluation of each state during the search.

The search starts using an informed initialization strategy, which provides a more structured starting point than a pure random assignment. The system follows a process where it chooses random unlit cells and places bulbs one by one until every white square on the board is illuminated. This approach also guarantees that no two bulbs look at each other at the beginning of the search, which is a big advantage. This results in a high quality starting configuration that ensures that the entire board is lit, even though some numerical clues might be violated.

The progress to the solution is measured by a cost function that the agent works to minimize. The function acts as a scoring system where the solution has a cost of zero and each violation increases the score. The agent is penalized for every white cell that remains unlit, for every instance where a numbered black cell has the wrong number of adjacent bulbs, and when there are bulbs shining on each other:

\[\ C(s) = \sum (Unlit White Cells)+\sum_ {c  \in  Clues}| n_c - a_c| +\sum (Bulb Collisions),\]
where for every numbered black cell \textit{c}, $n_c$ is the required number of bulbs and $a_c$ is  the actual number of adjacent bulbs. With informed initialization, search initially starts with zero bulb collisions.

\subsection{Hill Climbing}

This implementation utilizes a steepest-ascent logic combined with a "greedy" neighbor selection process. In each step, the algorithm samples 30 random neighbors from the current state using the 3-action model. The agent only transitions to a neighbor if it strictly improves the fitness (lowers the cost). If the best neighbor is not better than the current state, the agent identifies this as a local optimum. To escape these local optima, the solver immediately triggers a random restart, generating a completely new initial state and beginning the climb again until a solution is found or the timer expires. 

\subsection{Simulated Annealing}

The simulated annealing solver improves Hill Climbing by allowing the agent to occasionally accept moves that actually make the board state worse. This logic is designed to prevent the solver from getting stuck in a local dead end where no single move looks like an improvement, but a solution still exists in a different part of the search space. On each step, the agent proposes exactly one random change using the 3-action model. If this change lowers the cost function, it is always accepted immediately. However, if the change increases the cost, the solver calculates a probability based on a "temperature" value to decide whether to take the move anyway.  As the search goes on, this temperature gradually drops, which makes the agent selective about accepting bad moves. On early stages, when the temperature is high, the agent very frequently jumps to different configurations to explore the board. As the temperature drops, it becomes much stricter, eventually settling into a final arrangement and becoming more like regular hill climbing. If the temperature falls below a minimum threshold without a solution being found, the solver performs a "reheat". This resets the temperature to the starting high point and generates a completely new board configuration, allowing the search to begin again until a perfect arrangement is discovered.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

Comparing five solvers needs many puzzles, so a generator was written. It builds each
puzzle starting from a solution. First it places walls at a chosen density and mirrors
them through the centre of the board, because published puzzles usually look symmetric.
Then it builds a valid bulb placement: it repeatedly picks a random cell that is still
unlit and puts a bulb there. An unlit cell is outside the line of sight of every bulb
already placed, so this step can never create a conflict, and it stops when the whole
board is lit. Each wall then gets, with some probability, the number of bulbs next to it
in this placement. Finally the bulbs are removed. The result always has a solution,
because the placement it was built from is one. It is not always the only solution, and
this point comes back in Chapter~\ref{chap:results}.

Three presets control the densities. Easy boards have 25\% walls and a number on every
wall. Medium boards have 18\% walls and numbers on 80\% of them. Hard boards have 12\%
walls and numbers on 55\% of them. The names describe the effort a person needs, because
fewer clues make the chain of steps longer.

Five board sizes were used ($7\times7$, $10\times10$, $14\times14$, $18\times18$ and
$25\times25$), together with the three presets and five seeded puzzles in each
combination. This gives 75 different puzzles. Every solver was given exactly the same 75
puzzles, and every run was given five seconds, so the sweep contains 375 runs. The
generator depends only on the board size, the preset and the seed, and the two random
solvers use the same seed. Any run can therefore be repeated from those three values, and
the puzzles do not need to be stored.

Each run records whether it succeeded, the time taken, the number of search nodes (or, for
local search, the number of states checked), the number of conflicts, and the number of
assignments made by propagation. Local search runs also record the lowest cost reached,
which shows how close a failed run came to a solution. A run counts as successful only if
the validator confirms that the returned placement satisfies $C_1$, $C_2$ and $C_3$. The
result is not compared with a stored answer, because a puzzle can have several valid
solutions and any of them is correct.

\subsection{The effect of inference}

Figure~\ref{fig:scaling} shows how far apart the three backtracking solvers are. Measured
in search nodes, the gap between the naive solver and the two informed ones grows with the
board size. It is about seven times at $7\times7$, forty times at $10\times10$ and one
hundred and sixty times at $14\times14$. Inference does not simply remove a fixed amount
of work. It changes how fast the cost grows.

The clearest single result comes from the published $7\times7$ puzzle of
Pulles~\cite{analysis_akari}. The naive solver needs 308 search nodes to solve it and
forward checking needs 12. Full inference needs none at all: 41 propagation steps produce
the whole solution without a single branching decision. This fits the way such puzzles are
made, because a published puzzle is designed so that a person can solve it step by step,
and the propagation rules do the same steps automatically.

This effect is much rarer on the generated puzzles. Only 3 of the 15 boards at $7\times7$,
and none above $14\times14$, were solved without any search. A likely reason is that the
generator only guarantees a solution, not a single solution. Its boards have fewer
constraints, so less can be worked out from them.

\subsection{Complete search against local search}

Figure~\ref{fig:paradigms} compares all five solvers on hard boards. Simulated annealing
was better than hill climbing at every size. It solved 19 of the 25 hard puzzles against
11, and 45 of all 75 against 21. This repeats the main result of Sun, Browning and
Perera~\cite{light_up_ai} on different puzzles and with a separate implementation.

The way each solver fails is as interesting as the counts. The cost function reports how
many violations remain, so a failed run still shows how close it came. Hill climbing
finished its failed runs with 13 violations left on average, and simulated annealing with
only 5.5. In one twenty-second run on a hard $14\times14$ board, hill climbing restarted
12{,}496 times and never removed the last violation. This is a local optimum in its
simplest form: every possible move made the board worse, so a solver that accepts only
improvements could not move at all. Simulated annealing escapes because it sometimes
accepts a worse state.

    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig2_paradigms.png}
    \caption{Percentage of hard instances solved within the budget, by
    board size, for all five solvers.}
    \label{fig:paradigms}
\end{figure}

Two limitations of local search should be stated clearly. Neither solver can prove that a
board has no solution, because both can only report that they did not find one. And within
this budget, complete search with inference was better than both of them at every size
tested.

\subsection{What makes an instance hard}

The presets change the density of walls and clues, and denser boards were expected to be
easier for every solver. Figure~\ref{fig:difficulty} shows that this is not what happened.
At $14\times14$ hill climbing solved none of the easy or medium puzzles, but it solved
some of the hard ones. At $10\times10$ the pattern is even stronger: nothing on easy or
medium, and all five hard puzzles. Simulated annealing behaves the same way, solving 19 of
25 hard puzzles but only 13 of 25 easy ones. The informed backtracking solvers prefer the
opposite: forward checking solved all 25 easy puzzles and 22 hard ones.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{fig3_difficulty.png}
    \caption{Median solving time by difficulty preset at $14\times14$. A
    missing bar means that no run of that solver was solved within the
    budget.}
    \label{fig:difficulty}
\end{figure}

The reason is that a clue means different things to different solvers. For a solver that
reasons about constraints, a clue is information. It is exactly what the propagation rules
of Section~\ref{sec:csp} use, so more clues make the board easier. For a local search
solver, which can only score a complete board, each clue is one more penalty term in the
cost function. Dense boards therefore have more local optima and are harder.

This is not caused by problem size, because the size moves the other way: at $14\times14$
the hard preset leaves about 172 white cells, while the easy preset leaves about 146. The
presets predict how hard a puzzle is for a person, which depends on how long the chain of
steps is. They do not predict how hard it is for a solver.

\subsection{Sensitivity to the time budget}

A five-second budget is a free choice, so the whole sweep was repeated with ten seconds on
exactly the same puzzles. Almost nothing changed. The naive solver gained three puzzles,
forward checking and simulated annealing gained two each, full inference gained none
because it already solved everything, and hill climbing gained none at all
(Figure~\ref{fig:budget}). No puzzle was lost, which also confirms that the runs are
properly seeded and repeatable. More time helps a solver that is only slow, and does not
help a solver that is stuck. The differences above therefore come from the design of the
solvers, not from the chosen budget.

\subsection{Limitations}

Three points limit the numbers above. First, only five puzzles were used per combination
of size and difficulty. This is enough for the totals, but one puzzle changes a single
cell of Table~\ref{tab:solvesize} by twenty points, so the single cells should be read as
a rough guide only.

Second, all medians use solved runs only. This hides the failed runs. The median node
count of the naive solver drops between $14\times14$ and $18\times18$, but not because the
solver got better. Only the easiest puzzles of the larger size were solved, and only those
went into the median. Solve rate and search effort must therefore always be read together.

Third, all times were measured on one machine running CPython. The comparison between
solvers is still valid, but the times in milliseconds are not.

\section{Conclusion}

Five ways of solving the same problem were compared under the same conditions, and the
comparison gave a clear order. Inference was the most important factor. Adding propagation
to backtracking changed a solver that failed on most large boards into one that solved
every generated puzzle, and on a published puzzle it removed the need to search at all.
The gain grows with the board size, so inference changes how fast the cost grows and not
only how large it is.

Three results were not expected. Fewer search nodes do not always mean less time, because
propagation must be paid for at every node and only pays back when there is something to
work out. Simulated annealing was much better than hill climbing, which confirms earlier
work, and the way hill climbing failed shows the local optimum problem very clearly: it
stopped one violation away from a solution and stayed there through twelve thousand
restarts. Most surprising of all, sparse boards were easier for the solvers than dense
ones, which shows that the density presets describe human difficulty and not search
difficulty.

Finally, doubling the time budget changed almost nothing. This result gives the most
confidence in the others, because it shows that the differences come from how the solvers
are built and not from how long they were allowed to run.

\section{Possible Future work}

Three directions follow from the limitations above. First, the generator guarantees that a
puzzle has a solution, but not that the solution is the only one. Adding the uniqueness
check described by Pulles~\cite{analysis_akari} would test the idea that boards with a
single solution allow much more to be worked out.

Second, the puzzle could be solved as a satisfiability problem. The propagation rules used
here are already a form of unit propagation, so writing a CNF encoding and a DPLL solver
would allow a clause-based version to be compared with the constraint-based one. The
solver would be written from scratch rather than taken from a library, as earlier work
did.

Third, the presets describe human difficulty rather than search difficulty. They could be
set again using the search effort measured here, which would also give a controlled way to
produce puzzles of a chosen hardness.
